Strength · Heracles Training Load

How a strength session is scored.

Heracles Training Load scores a strength session from its sets: the weight, the reps, your body weight and the most you have lifted on each exercise. One formula for everyone, built from published research and public data, on the same scale as COROS's Training Load for a run. This page sets out all of it.

Why not heart rate

COROS's Training Load is a TRIMP: every minute counts by how far your heart rate sits into its reserve, the range between resting and maximum. That suits a run or a ride, where heart rate follows the effort. Lifting is different. A hard set lasts half a minute, heart rate climbs after it and falls through the rest, and the muscles do work the heart barely sees.

PMData, a public dataset of people who rated each session beside the heart rate their watch recorded, shows the size of the gap. At the same rating of 5 to 7 out of 10, its weights sessions earned 0.55 to 0.8 load a minute from heart rate, while steady endurance sessions earned 1.3 to 1.9. Read by heart rate, lifting counts two to three times less than it felt. Your week's load, Base Fitness and recommended range all inherit that.

The idea

A minute of lifting at an effort weighs what a steady minute at the same effort weighs. So the formula works out how hard each set was, from how close it went to failure, and charges its minutes at the load COROS gives a steady effort that feels the same. Being on a run's scale follows from that; nothing is tuned to make it so. 

Heracles TL = max( COROS TL,
                   Σ f(set RPE) × set minutes  +  f(1.5) × minutes between sets )

f(RPE) = h × 0.2445 × e^(3.411 × h)        h = 0.48 + 0.031 × RPE

f is COROS's own TRIMP weighting of a minute; h is the share of heart-rate reserve a steady effort at that rating usually reaches. Every constant is published or fitted on many people, none on you.

The formula, step by step

1. What the set moves

L = weight lifted + α × body weight − assistance

α is the share of your body the working muscles move, from force plates (push-up variations, Ebben 2011) and body segment masses (de Leva 1996). It is 0 for a lift where the bar is the whole load. A weighted squat or lunge counts the bar and most of you.

A Hevy movement takes the share of the COROS movement it is, by name, and an assisted pull-up or dip has its assistance taken off the body. A movement the app does not know counts as bodyweight (α 0.6) until a set of it carries a weight. Body weight is the one on your COROS profile on the session's day: the app keeps each change, since COROS holds only the current one.

2. What you can lift

C = best, over 120 days, of  L ÷ %1RM(reps + reps in reserve)

Your capacity on a lift is the one-rep max your sets imply. Every set of 1 to 20 reps in the last 120 days, the session's own included, gives an estimate, and the best one stands. A set with no RPE logged is taken to have stopped two reps short of failure: lifters judge themselves closer to failure than they are, by one to two reps with experience and four to five without (Steele 2017, Refalo 2024). A Hevy set marked failure counts as zero, and an RPE of 5 or more logged in Hevy says how far from failure that set was.

  • A new best more than 15% above the old one needs a second set of the session to back it: one set that far ahead is usually a miscounted rep.
  • A bodyweight movement never reads below what a recreational adult of your weight manages, with strength growing as body mass to the power 0.67 (Jaric 2002).
  • Warm-up sets never count. Capacity only looks back, so a new best never rewrites an old session.

3. How close to failure

I = min(1, L ÷ C)
reps in reserve = reps to failure at I − reps done

Reps to failure at a share of the one-rep max come from Nuzzo et al. 2024, a meta-regression of 952 tests on 7,289 people in 269 studies, in which sex, age and training status did not clearly move them. The app reads between the rows.

4. How hard the set was

Reps in reserve become an RPE on the scale lifters use (Zourdos 2016, Helms 2016): one rep left is 9, three is 7, six is 5, and past that the effort eases off gently. An RPE you log in Hevy replaces the prediction.

Reps lost to fatigue. Within one exercise, a set ends no further from failure than an earlier set at a weight no heavier. Lifters hold their effort across sets; when the reps drop at the same weight, that is fatigue, not reps held back. Without this rule, 25 kg × 17, 13, 11, 10 would read RPE 8, 5, 4.1 and 3.8. With it, all four read 8.

Holds, a plank or a side plank, score by time held against your longest hold (or a hard hold for a recreational adult): RPE = 2 + 8 × the share.

5. How long the set counts

A set counts its work and the rest after it, up to three minutes: the time it holds you at that effort. COROS clocks both in its laps. Hevy keeps no clock for a set of reps, so a Hevy set counts 3 seconds a rep, and the time the session's sets leave is shared between them as rest, still up to three minutes each. A session reads the same whichever app logged it. With no session length either, each set rests 90 seconds.

6. What a minute weighs

Each set's minutes are charged at f(RPE): the load COROS gives a minute of steady exercise at the share of heart-rate reserve that effort usually reaches.

7. The session

Add up the sets, then the time between them (the warm-up, changing lifts, stretching, a watch left running) at RPE 1.5, very easy. Warm-up and cool-down steps, rests, drills and stretches held three minutes or more belong to that time. The result never falls below COROS's own figure for the session: COROS's part is the cardio, the rest is the muscular work.

A worked example

A 75 kg athlete whose best bench press in the last 120 days was 80 kg × 6. Two reps short of failure, that is about 96 kg for one rep. Today: three sets of bench press at 70 kg and three sets of pull-ups, each set 30 seconds with 2 minutes' rest, after a 6-minute warm-up: 21 minutes in all.

A workout logged only in Hevy has no COROS figure, so it is scored from its sets alone, and has no load at all with COROS TL chosen.

Planned sessions

COROS usually plans a strength session at a load of 0, which leaves a plan's weekly load short by every gym day. A strength session on your calendar or in a plan is estimated with the same formula: your capacity and weight today, 3 seconds a rep and the planned rest (90 seconds when the plan gives none), the rest of its planned length at RPE 1.5. A weighted lift planned with no weight takes your usual RPE, and a session with no readable steps takes your usual RPE for its planned length.

What changes when you choose it

Heracles TL is a setting: on the Strength screen, Settings, then Training load formula. COROS TL is the default. With Heracles TL chosen:

  • Every strength session's load, and every total that adds them up, on Strength, Overview, the Calendar, the Training Library and Activities. Each set shows its predicted RPE and load, and the body map gains Load.
  • COROS's fitness figures, by COROS's own arithmetic on the daily load.
  • Coach and AI Plan read it beside COROS's figure, and plan the weekly load in it.
Load Impact       += (day's load − Load Impact) ÷ 7
Base Fitness      += (day's load − Base Fitness) ÷ 42
Intensity Trend    = Load Impact ÷ Base Fitness
Recommended week   = Base Fitness on the Sunday before × 7, up to 30% more

These are linear in the daily load, so the app adds what Heracles adds each day to COROS's own figures rather than rebuilding your history. Where a figure differs from the COROS app, hovering it shows COROS's own.

What stays COROS's: other sports, recovery and recovery time, training effect, and everything on your watch. The app never writes Heracles TL back to COROS.

Where the numbers come from

  • The effort curve was fitted on PMData (Thambawita et al. 2020). Each session rating was matched to the Fitbit exercise it rates, the TRIMP of its heart rate read back to a steady share of reserve, with each person's measured maximum and daily resting heart rate. Across 429 steady sessions of 14 people: h = 0.481 + 0.0310 × RPE. Age-predicted maximum heart rate (Nes 2013) in place of the measured one gives 0.486 + 0.0312. Leaving each person out in turn moves the load a minute at RPE 8 between 2.01 and 2.29.
  • The curve against the data, average load a minute at each rating: RPE 5, 1.35 on the curve and 1.32 measured; RPE 7, 1.84 on the curve and 1.86 measured; RPE 8, 2.13 on the curve and 1.95 measured.
  • Weights sessions in the same data: at RPE 5 to 7, heart rate gave them 0.55 to 0.8 a minute, against 1.3 to 1.9 for steady endurance at the same rating.
  • On real training: over the developer's own ten strength sessions read by set, Heracles TL came to about 2.4 times COROS's figure (median), in line with the two- to threefold gap in PMData.

Only two constants come from the data, and the app ships none of the data itself. The fit is a script in the app's source, ready to run again on a larger public dataset.

Known limits

  • Capacity is read from your own sets. Your best set in 120 days is taken as two reps short of failure. If you often train to failure, those sets read 16 to 34% light; if you rarely push hard, a little heavy. Logging RPE in Hevy corrects it.
  • Rest counts at the set's effort. The same sets with longer rests score higher, though short rests often feel harder. That is how session RPE has always worked (Foster 2001); the fatigue rule makes up part of it.
  • One bridge is assumed, not yet measured: that a set's RPE, judged by reps in reserve, matches a whole session's rating on the 0 to 10 scale.
  • The curve is a typical person. If your heart rate runs high for an effort, your own runs read heavier than lifting at the same RPE.
  • PMData is small: 14 recreational athletes in Norway, with Fitbits on the wrist.
  • COROS does not know Heracles TL. Recovery time and everything on your watch still under-count the gym.
